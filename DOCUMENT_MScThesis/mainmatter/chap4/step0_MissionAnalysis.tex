\subsection{Implementation}

\subsubsection{Map extraction and preprocessing}
% OSM data: which layers (buildings + heights, roads, open areas)
% reprojection to UTM (metric coordinates), clipping to the area of interest
First, it is necessary to conduct a territorial analysis of the area above which the mission is supposed to take place. This implies processing OpenStreetMap (OSM) data extracted for the relevant geographical area of Turin. The OSM file is usually a plain XML document containing three types of elements, namely nodes, ways and relations. Nodes are individual points with a latitude and a longitude, ways are lines or polygons connecting the nodes, and relations are groups of ways that together form larger features, like a river.

A code was necessary to extract this information and classify it, so that the territory of Turin could be divided into five different categories.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Water, including waterways such as the Po river or the Dora Riparia, which are described as ways with a thickness, going from 60~m for the Po to 18~m and 9~m for smaller waterways [?].
  \item Buildings, i.e. anything tagged with the \texttt{building} key in the OSM file, for example schools, churches, houses, hospitals, etc.
  \item Roads, i.e. the ways tagged as \texttt{highway} across the city. Any corridor [?] or railway line is also classified as such.
  \item Green/natural. The idea here was to classify all natural terrain, such as gardens, pitches and parks, but also grass, farmland, meadows, recreation grounds and woods, i.e. anything that has to do with nature.
  \item Ground, which is where everything else that is not in the first four categories falls. It is assigned to any cell that is still unclassified at the end of this block.
\end{enumerate}
In this way, all the individual features of the map are classified into these five categories.

After this initial classification, the map is built with a first conversion of the reference system, because in the OSM file the coordinates are in longitude and latitude, while we need them in meters. A UTM projection (zone 32N [EPSG:32632?]) is used to convert from one to the other. In particular, it makes the angular geometry planar, so that what was a globe becomes a plane, in a way that depends on the area of the globe in which it is. Since Turin is in zone 32, the coordinates are projected onto the plane of that zone and are now in meters, about 4990~km north of the equator and about 100~km west of the central meridian of the zone ($9^\circ$E). The bounding box measures approximately 4757~m (easting) $\times$ 6924~m (northing). At the current $500 \times 500$ resolution (250\,000 cells), each cell measures approximately 9.5~m $\times$ 13.8~m.

Once the OSM data is classified and everything is converted into UTM meters, the third step is to actually build the grid. A $500 \times 500$ grid is built, and each cell is assigned one of the five categories (ground, building, etc.) depending on what lies at its centre. If the centre falls on a tree, the cell is assigned to green/natural, if it falls on a building, to buildings, and so on. The idea is to simplify the process of building the actual grid, without considering the variation within the single cell.

Step~4 is then to visualise the grid as a map that can actually be analysed, as seen in Fig. whatever.

\subsubsection{Mission trajectory definition}
% route endpoints (hospitals), B-spline trajectory, cruise altitude and speed
The second step is to define the trajectory above the map produced earlier. The idea is that the waypoints of the trajectory, selected manually and set up in latitude and longitude in an external Excel file, are loaded and checked against the bounding box of the map, and any waypoint falling outside it is discarded. The waypoints are then converted to UTM meters (zone 32N, EPSG:32632), with the same transformation used for the map, and interpolated with a cubic B-spline using the \texttt{splprep} function of SciPy. No smoothing is applied ($s = 0$), so the curve passes exactly through every waypoint. The spline is parametric, i.e. $x$ and $y$ are both expressed as functions of a parameter $t \in [0, 1]$, and the curve is made of cubic polynomials joined at the waypoints with continuous first and second derivatives. If fewer than four valid waypoints are available, the code falls back to straight line segments ($k = 1$).

Then, points every 10~m along the curve are extracted through an arclength parametrisation. The spline is evaluated at 100\,000 equally spaced values of $t$, and the cumulative arclength is computed by summing the lengths of the straight segments between consecutive points. The values of $t$ that correspond to every 10~m of distance are found by linear interpolation on this cumulative arclength and saved, and the spline is evaluated at these values to give the points where the evaluation is conducted later. The same goes for the heading angle, computed at every sample point from the local direction of the trajectory and measured counterclockwise from East. The sample points are accurate because, for a total length of about 11~km, consecutive points of the dense evaluation are about 0.11~m apart, so there are about 90 of them for every sample point.

Finally, the spline is visualised on the map and the trajectory points are saved.

\subsubsection{Failure and fall model}
% failure scenario (where along the route, initial state at failure)
% equations of motion: gravity + drag (Cd, area, mass), integration scheme
% wind: included or not

The full model assumes that the payload is detached from the drone and then falls without propulsion or control, so the equations of motion that determine its trajectory include both the gravitational contribution and the aerodynamic contribution, the latter through a drag term. Given the air density $\rho$, the frontal area of the payload $S$ and the drag coefficient $C_d$, the drag force is
\begin{equation}
  F_d = c\,V^2, \qquad c = \frac{1}{2}\,\rho\,S\,C_d ,
  \label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the instantaneous speed of the payload.

For every combination of parameters, the drag constant $c$ is calculated first, and then the terminal velocity
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma = \sqrt{\frac{m\,g}{c}} ,
  \label{eq:terminal_velocity}
\end{equation}
i.e. the maximum vertical speed the payload can reach, at which drag balances gravity. For the payload considered here ($m = 1.5$~kg, $S = 0.04$~m$^2$, $C_d \approx 1.0$), $\Gamma \approx 24.5$~m/s, which is close to the cruise speed of the drone (about 23~m/s).

The descent is then divided into three phases. There is an upward phase, present only if the payload has an upward vertical velocity at separation. Then there is a descent in which the vertical velocity is lower than the horizontal velocity, which ends when the two become equal. Finally, there is the final descent from this crossing to the ground, in which the vertical velocity catches up and approaches $\Gamma$ while the horizontal velocity keeps decreasing. Each phase has its own approximate closed-form expressions for position and velocity~\cite{[key?]}, and the horizontal range is the sum of the distances covered in the three phases. The effect of the wind and of the drag is taken into account throughout [how does the wind enter the equations?].

The conditions at the moment of separation are not known exactly. For this reason the uncertain parameters are modelled as Gaussian distributions, each described by a mean $\mu$ and a standard deviation $\sigma$, and sampled randomly at each simulation, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:uncertain_params}.

\subsubsection{Monte Carlo impact loop}
% sampled variables + their distributions
% number of samples, convergence criterion
For each point of the trajectory calculated earlier, i.e. for every value $t^*$ corresponding to a distance of 10~m along the curve, a Monte Carlo simulation is run with 5000 combinations of parameters, selected randomly from the Gaussian distributions of Table~\ref{tab:uncertain_params}. The idea is that, once these parameters are chosen and the heading at that point is read from the trajectory, the function \texttt{impact\_point\_ballistic\_descent} is called, which calculates where each of these failure scenarios ends up, i.e. where the payload falls in case of that scenario. The function computes the horizontal range and the impact time of all 5000 scenarios at once and rotates the resulting cloud of impact points by the heading, so that it is oriented along the flight direction. Each impact point is then shifted by the wind drift $V_w\,t_{im}$ in the wind direction, assuming that the wind carries the payload at its own speed for the whole duration of the fall, and the cloud is finally translated to the UTM coordinates of the trajectory point.

The impact points are then counted on a grid of cells [$N_1 \times N_2$?] that are much smaller than the cells chosen to represent the terrain map, so the impact distribution is computed with a much higher accuracy. For each trajectory point this gives a local distribution, and the local distributions of all the points are summed up with equal weight. The sum is normalised over the total number of impacts that have happened, i.e. 5000 times the number of points on the trajectory. Each cell then contains the probability that, in case of a failure along the trajectory, the payload falls in that cell.

Finally, each impact cell is associated with the terrain category of the nearest cell of the terrain map, and the probabilities of all the cells belonging to the same category are summed up. In this way the total probability of impact on the ground, on water, on buildings and so on is obtained.
