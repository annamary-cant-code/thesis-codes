\section{Step 2 - Axial compression test (FEM)}\label{section:step2-fem-compression}
The quantity of interest for the calibration of the glass phial's mechanical behaviour is the \textit{tensile strength} \texttt{FT} of the glass material card in LS-DYNA (\kw{MAT\_GLASS}, corresponding to the material model \kw{MAT\_280}), which governs the failure load observed in the present FE model. As the model was designed to simulate as closely as possible the loading condition to which the phials were subjected in Step 1 of the workflow, the parameter \texttt{FT} is tuned iteratively until the failure force predicted by the FEM matches the value of 98.7\,N measured experimentally. The calibrated model is then used to characterise the behaviour of the same glass phials in a separate FE model in Step 3, aimed at predicting the maximum impact acceleration that the phials can withstand. This value is one of the key design parameters for sizing the airbag protection device. 

The model is built using \textit{explicit} time integration. The unit system used in the present model is as follows:

The current modelling choices are the result of several model iterations, carried out by trial and error, from a first implicit analysis (using Abaqus as a solver) to the present LS-DYNA model. These iterations are summarised in \cref{appendix:FEMiterations}.

\subsection{Implementation}
\subsubsection{Model setup and glass material modelling}
% geometry (full model), components, mesh, contact, boundary conditions

The model is composed of three parts, as shown in \cref{fig:fem-model-setup}: the \textit{base}, the \textit{crosshead} and the \textit{glass phial}. The numerical setup aims to imitate the experimental test, where the glass phial is compressed in the axial direction, i.e. the vertical (Y) direction. Two considerations need to be made regarding the construction of the model:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The glass phials virtual twin was reproduced on the computer-aided design (CAD) software SolidWorks by taking manual (destructive) measurements on the specimens offered by the Molinette Hospital. As the phials' geometry contains curvatures, fillets, domes, and bulges, using a simple Vernier calliper certainly lowers the fidelity of the virtual representation and induces challenges in the evaluation of the numerical results;
  \item The absence of the PETG holder in the present numerical model is a decision rooted in the risk of inaccurate or excessively complex modelling, as it was 3D printed with a reticular filling that would require a destructive intervention to be observed. The consequences of omitting the PETG adaptor in the FE model are another aspect that is explored and accounted for in the results presented later.
\end{enumerate}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chap4/step2-fem-phial/Fig_A_vertical_compression.png}
    \caption{FE model of the axial compression test in isometric (left) and front (right) view: the \textit{glass phial} is compressed between the fixed rigid \textit{base} and the rigid \textit{crosshead} moving in the $-$Y direction.}
    \label{fig:fem-model-setup}

Within the FE model, the crosshead and the base belong to the same super-part, named \textit{machine}, and they are meshed identically. Both are made of solid hexa elements with an average side length of 0.2\,mm, extruded to a thickness of 1\,mm in the vertical direction. In plan, they measure 18 x 18\,mm (base) and 14 x 14\,mm (crosshead). Both have enough surface area to cover the base and the dome of the glass phial, so that the breaking pressure and the reaction force from the base are applied correctly.

The two parts are modelled with a material that treats them as infinitely rigid bodies (\kw{MAT\_RIGID}, material model \kw{MAT\_020}), with only one free degree of freedom, in this case the displacement in the Y-direction. The base is then constrained in Y by a boundary condition that imposes a displacement of 0\,mm, while the crosshead moves downwards and applies pressure on the phial in a quasi-static regime. The numerical workaround of leaving the Y direction free and then imposing a zero-displacement boundary condition on the base makes it possible to measure the reaction force exerted on it in the direction of the degree of freedom that was chosen as liable. With a fully constrained rigid body, this measurement would not be so straightforward, needing a slightly different procedure. The material card applied to the machine's parts can be found in \cref{lst:mat-rigid}.

The glass phial is meshed with shell elements with \texttt{ELFORM = 2}\footnote{Reduced-integration elements with one in-plane integration point and through-thickness points chosen manually, with the default of two. No hourglass control implemented.} of thickness 0.4\,mm, with 5 integration points along the thickness. The structured mesh has a regular size of 0.1\,mm and is mostly composed of quads, with trias where needed to follow the shape, so that it keeps the curvatures of the dome and the fillets of the rounded-corner geometry. The material used for the glass phial is shown in \cref{lst:mat-glass-raw}, which highlights the \textit{tensile strength} \texttt{FT} as the parameter to fine-tune the numerical prediction of the phial's failure load. The card has several parameters of interest that govern the post-fracture behaviour, which is not of interest for the present analysis as the objective is to identify the material's point of brittle failure, aligned with the broader aim to avoid medicinal dispersion during an impact-failure event. However, an informed attempt to model glass behaviour with an acceptable degree of fidelity in both the pre- and post-fracture regimes was made by setting material parameters based on a separate literature search across worked examples of \kw{MAT\_GLASS} implementations \cite{DCFEA2025} and the LS-DYNA user manual \cite[p.~1989-2001]{LSDYNA_KW_II}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{RHO}: density, 2.6E-06\,kg/mm$^3$ (2600\,kg/m$^3$);
  \item \texttt{E}: Young's modulus, 70\,GPa, a relatively well-established value for commercial soda-lime glass, adopted here as an approximation of the Type I borosilicate glass of the phials (see \cref{section:step1-test}), whose elastic properties differ only slightly. As the manufacturer of the glass phials is unknown, this value carries some uncertainty;
  \item \texttt{PR}: Poisson's ratio, 0.23, chosen within a range between 0.20 to 0.25 for soda-lime glass, with the same approximation and uncertainty as for \texttt{E};
  \item \texttt{ILAW}: flag to choose the degradation procedure (among two options, 0 and 1) when critical stress is reached. The default value of 0 signifies that the family of parameters relative to \texttt{NCYCR} (found in lines 8-9 of the material card) have to be defined;
  \item \texttt{FMOD}: flag to choose between failure criteria (among six options). The default value of 0 is chosen, selecting a \textit{Rankine maximum stress} criterion\footnote{The \textit{Rankine Maximum Stress} criterion consists in bounding principal stresses, $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, between tensile strength, $f_t$, and compressive strength, $f_c$, as follows: $-f_c < \{\sigma_1, \sigma_2\} < f_t$. It is the least complex maximum stress criterion available in LS-DYNA.};
  \item \texttt{FT}: tensile strength, knob to fine-tune the glass phial's failure load, expressed in GPa. Common values found in literature span from 40\,MPa to 70\,MPa. 
  \item \texttt{FC}: compressive strength, usually one order of magnitude greater than the tensile strength in glass, here arbitrarily chosen to 1\,GPa to guarantee tensile-strength driven failure when fine-tuning the parameter \texttt{FT};
  \item \texttt{FTSCL}: scale factor for the tensile strength (where the default = 1.0). \texttt{FT}$_\text{mod}$ = \texttt{FTSCL} $\times$ \texttt{FT}, thus, in this case, the glass' actual tensile strength needs to be scaled by the chosen factor of \texttt{FTSCL} = 1.2. An effective value of tensile strength \texttt{FT}$_\text{mod} >$ \texttt{FT} implies that the tensile strength drops to its original value of \texttt{FT} as soon as the first crack happens in the phial. Although it adds numerical complexity, having \texttt{FTSCL} $>$ 1.0 can be helpful in modelling high-force peaks in impact events according to the LS-DYNA user manual, which suits the intended application of all the FE models produced in this thesis;   
  \item \texttt{SFSTI} and \texttt{SFSTR}: scaling factors for elastic stiffness and stress after failure, respectively, to be applied at the end of the post-crack softening. It dictates how much residual stress the element can hold after it has been damaged, scaling the post-crack strength and stiffness down to \texttt{SF-XXX} $\times$ \texttt{FT} or \texttt{E}. In this case, \texttt{SFSTI} = \texttt{SFSTR} = 0.01 means that both stiffness and stress are reduced to 1\% of their values at failure; 
  \item \texttt{ICRIN}: flag for crack strain initialisation. The default value of 0 indicates that initial crack strain is the strain reached at failure;
  \item \texttt{NCYCR}: number of cycles in which the stress is reduced to \texttt{SFSTR} $\times$ failure stress, in this case chosen to 50.
  \item \texttt{NIPF}: number of failed through-thickness integration points needed to label the element as failed, in this case chosen as 5 to consider all through-thickness integration points set in the phial's shell elements;
  \item \texttt{EPSCR}: the effective critical strain to trigger element deletion, which can be useful to eliminate highly distorted elements. However, this value was set to the extremely high value of 0.1 to purposefully avoid premature element deletion and having to implement an internal surface generation criterion for contact modelling.
  \item \texttt{CTRACK}: flag for crack tracking algorithm, set to the non-default value of 1 to track cracks. 
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Boundary conditions}
The phial simply rests on the base's surface. Its interaction with the base and the crosshead is governed by the contact card \kw{CONTACT\_AUTOMATIC\_SINGLE\_SURFACE\_ID}, the most general contact model in LS-DYNA, as shown in \cref{lst:contact-single-surface}. It allows all surfaces to come in contact with each other without defining single master-slave pairs. The tangential behaviour is defined by a friction coefficient of 0.3 for both static (\texttt{FS}) and dynamic (\texttt{FD}) friction. The phial is liable and only feels the reaction force from below and the force applied by the moving crosshead.

A velocity-control boundary condition is applied to the crosshead in the vertical direction Y to induce a total displacement of approximately 0.2\,mm. Velocity is applied with an initial ramp that scales up to a constant negative velocity of 0.0266\,mm/ms. The choice of imposing the motion of the crosshead through a velocity boundary condition, rather than the more usual displacement boundary condition, is rooted in the idea of emulating the experiment, in which the test machine moves the crosshead at a constant, relatively slow speed of 1 to 3\,mm/min. Furthermore, velocity cannot be applied as a step as an instantaneous jump from zero to the final velocity would correspond to an infinite acceleration at the beginning of the analysis. In an explicit simulation, this impulse would be transmitted to the phial through the contact as a numerically unwelcome spike in the reaction force, followed by spurious oscillations that could potentially even trigger premature glass failure. With the initial ramp, the velocity increases linearly, so the acceleration remains finite and non-zero only during the ramp. The application velocity is low enough to avoid a fully dynamic regime, as explained in the modal analysis section, but still higher than in the actual experiment, as applying a similar loading speed would increase the runtime excessively due to the stability condition imposed on the timestep.

The first elastic mode (eigenmode 7) is of particular relevance for the present analysis. It is a bending mode, with a natural frequency of about 8.179\,kHz, and its period $T_1$ is the characteristic time of the structure's dynamic response:

\begin{equation}
T_1 = 1/f_1 \approx 122~\mu\mathrm{s}
\end{equation}

If the load were applied over a time of the same order as $T_1$, the structure's inertia would contribute to the response and the test would fall in the dynamic regime. Therefore, to keep the simulation quasi-static, the load application time must be at least ten times $T_1$, i.e.\ above about 1.22\,ms. For this reason, the crosshead was driven by a velocity curve that ramps up over 5\,ms to a constant value and then holds it for the remaining 5\,ms of the simulation. The ramp time is therefore about 40 times the period of the first elastic mode, well inside the quasi-static range. 

\subsubsection{Geometric imperfections}
The phials tested experimentally showed a large dispersion in their strength properties, as well as in their failure modes. This phenomenon is usually ascribed to the loosely controlled manufacturing process (mentioned in \cref{section:step1-test}) which naturally introduces geometric imperfections. These imperfections are not influential in the context of the medical applications for which the phials were designed, but are certainly exposed in destructive testing such as what was adopted in Step 1. For these reasons, it was necessary to introduce initial defects into the FE model by perturbing the phial's geometry. Two options were considered:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Perturbing the nodes along a primarily bending (see eigenmode 18 in \cref{fig:phial-modes}) mode shape from the modal analysis, as to introduce a specific defect of controlled amplitude (measured in mm) over the nominal geometry;
  \item Leaving the geometry unchanged and rotating the phial by $1^\circ$ about its centroid in the YZ-plane, which introduces a misalignment between the phial axis and the loading direction (as shown in \cref{fig:phial-misalignment})
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/chap4/step2-fem-phial/Fig_D_tilted_phial.png}
  \caption{Phial rotated by $1^\circ$ about its centroid in the YZ-plane, introducing a misalignment between the phial axis and the loading direction.}
  \label{fig:phial-misalignment}
\end{figure}

After a series of iterations verifying the numerically observed failure mode, the second option was chosen as it was found that this misalignment triggers an additional, hybrid failure mode, which had also been observed experimentally. The results presented later therefore confirm a correlation between initial deviations from the nominal geometry and the different failure modes observed in the tests.


\subsubsection{Requested outputs}
The monitored outputs are the displacement of the crosshead and the displacement of the glass node on top of the dome (monitored through the \texttt{NODOUT} card), the reaction forces on both the crosshead and the base (\texttt{RCFORC} card), added mass/energy data (\texttt{GLSTAT, MATSUM} cards), and the \texttt{MAT\_GLASS} \textit{Extra History Variable \#1}, with an index ($D \in [0, 1]$) stored in the \texttt{d3plot} contour file to visualise the damage progression and crack contour patterns across the glass phial. To obtain results comparable with the experimental campaign, a force-displacement curve was extracted from each run of the simulation, using the reaction force on the crosshead and the displacement of the glass node at the top of the phial dome (considering its displacement only until its eventual brittle failure). The objective is to reproduce the experimentally-observed brittle failure, in which the glass behaves linearly elastically up to fracture, with no plastic deformation before it breaks.


\subsubsection{Calibration procedure}
As shown in the \cref{lst:mat-glass-raw}, the tensile strength \texttt{FT} is the parameter used as a calibration knob to adjust the breaking point of the phial until it matches the failure load value measured experimentally at 98.7\,N (5th percentile of the Batch-A Weibull distribution of failure loads). Therefore, several simulations were run iteratively testing different values of \texttt{FT} until the simulated phial broke within a 2\% tolerance from the experimental failure load. The resulting value of \texttt{FT} should therefore be read as a calibrated model parameter rather than as the actual tensile strength of the glass, since it also absorbs the effects of mesh size, imperfections and misalignment. This calibrated material model characterises the phial and is used as input to the next step of the workflow.

\subsubsection{Stiffness verification}
A further check needed to assess the reliability of the FE model is the comparison between the experimental stiffness of the phials and the numerical one, where stiffness is intended as the slope of the load-displacement curve of the glass phial relative to this specific load case. As highlighted in \cref{fig:step2-stiffness-overlay}, the model alone has a higher stiffness than the one measured experimentally, with a ratio of $\approx$ 3.7 for mode A and $\approx$ 2.8 for mode AD, with respect to the median measured stiffness of each mode. The additional test campaign on the PETG adaptor documented in \cref{sec:step1-results} showed that the holder cannot be treated as infinitely rigid, and that its compliance must be taken into account when measuring the displacement imposed on the phial. The two are therefore combined as stiffnesses in series:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:series_stiffness}
  \frac{1}{K_\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{K_\text{FEM}} + \frac{1}{K_\text{PETG}} ,
\end{equation}

where $K_\text{FEM}$ is the stiffness of the phial model and $K_\text{PETG}$ the stiffness of the PETG adaptor. Approximating the stiffnesses to the constant values of 3655\,N/mm (mode A) or 2767\,N/mm (mode AD) and 2037\,N/mm, the equivalent stiffness is 1308\,N/mm and 1173\,N/mm respectively. Since the measured response of the PETG adaptor is not perfectly linear, these values are only a first estimate. For this reason, in the post-processing of the FEM data, the same combination is applied at every load point: for a given load $F$, the displacement computed by the FE model and the displacement of the PETG adaptor measured experimentally are added together as in the following equation:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:series_displacement}
  \delta_\text{eq}(F) = \delta_\text{FEM}(F) + \delta_\text{PETG}(F) 
\end{equation}

which gives the corrected curves shown as thick lines in \cref{fig:step2-stiffness-overlay}. With this normalisation, the equivalent stiffness of the model breaking in mode A and in mode AD falls into the experimental range highlighted in \cref{fig:breaking-mode-pie}. This comparison confirms the consistency of the model with the experiment and validates the stiffness of the FE model, at least in terms of \textit{range} of values. For mode AD, however, this agreement should be read with caution, as the experimental range is based on only six phials and its upper limit of 1321\,N/mm comes from the single phial of Batch B in the group. Considering Batch A alone (five phials, 259\,N/mm - 1049\,N/mm), the equivalent stiffness of mode AD, 1154\,N/mm, lies about 10\% above the experimental range, whereas that of mode A, 1251\,N/mm, remains within it (318\,N/mm - 1506\,N/mm).

However, these results are still affected by the limitation mentioned earlier, i.e. the geometry of the phial, and especially its thickness, is not exactly known. A campaign on different thicknesses was carried out and is reported in \cref{appendix:FEMiterations}. It did not yield any significant results, except for confirming slight changes in the overall stiffness, not large enough to radically change the results presented here. A summary of these observations is given in \cref{tab:step2-stiffness-summary}.

\subsubsection{Tensile strength calibration} 
Previous observations confer the FE model enough reliability to conduct the tensile strength finetuning process. Analogous to the load case of two springs in series as illustrated above, the load needed for the phial's glass to locally reach the critical stress remains unchanged compared to the experimentally measured value. Owing to the higher stiffness of the FE model, the critical load is merely reached over a smaller displacement, leading to the results presented in \cref{fig:step2-ft-calibration}. 

The two graphs in \cref{fig:step2-ft-calibration} show the load-displacement curves for mode A and mode AD as parameter \texttt{FT} varies. Each curve shows an initial soft branch indicating a transient phase in which machine-phial contact fully engages, followed by a linear elastic behaviour up to the brittle failure point. Since the phial's stiffness is naturally independent of \texttt{FT}, the failure points of the different curves lie along the same slope, at increasing loads for increasing values of \texttt{FT}. For mode A, a value of \texttt{FT} = 60\,MPa gives a breaking load of 96.9\,N, compared with 98.7\,N established experimentally. While at first sight mode AD points to a different \texttt{FT} value to match the 98.7\,N failure threshold, it can be useful to isolate the load-displacement curves with \texttt{FT} = 60\,MPa for both mode A and mode AD, as illustrated in \cref{fig:step2-ft-calibration-overlay}. The FE model captures the hybrid failure mode AD remarkably well: initial fracture occurs at the base due to tilting-induced stress concentrations, but the structure retains partial load-bearing capacity until ultimate failure occurs at a load comparable to the dome failure in mode A. This aligns with the first-hand laboratory experience concerning the hybrid mode AD, which was characterised by distinct acoustic emissions: a minor energy release during initial base fracture, followed by a much larger release upon final dome collapse.

Overall, it is possible to conclude that the calibrated value of \texttt{FT} = 60\,MPa successfully reproduces the experimental behaviour for Batch A, closely aligning with the $5^{\text{th}}$ percentile estimate derived in \cref{section:step1-test}. While this value lies at the boundary between the bending strengths reported for annealed (21.2 - 58.6\,MPa) and heat-strengthened (54.9 - 167.0\,MPa) float glass \cite{veer2009strength}, using a value from such a low percentile suggests that the true material strength is likely higher. The fact that a low tensile strength is required to match the experimental load suggests that the modelled phial geometry may be overly thin, as a thicker geometry would offer greater compressive resistance. The combination of thin wall and low breaking load is therefore a generally conservative reproduction of phial behaviour, which suits the purposes of this workflow. 

The results also show a remarkable sensitivity to initial defects and to the variability of the phials' geometry. For this reason, the quantitative predictive capability of the model is limited, but the qualitative indication is clear: it is fundamental to improve ways of characterising medical payloads. Coming from a different industry, pharmaceutical phials are produced and certified according to standards that focus on requirements such as chemical resistance and dimensional tolerances, rather than on the structural behaviour under extreme impact loads (outside the context of ordinary transportation). As a consequence, the mechanical data needed in an aerospace design process are generally not available, and studying the phials with the means typically used for aerospace structures makes their behaviour hard to predict.

Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as a rigorous material characterisation. The testing conditions were non-standard, including a custom adaptor that required stiffness calibration and a batch-to-batch variability stemming directly from differences in pharmaceutical phial manufacturing parameters. Furthermore, this evaluation was strictly limited to axial compression, omitting other critical loading cases such as pure bending or direct tension, which would typically exhibit even higher scatter in failure strength. Ultimately, this testing served as a pragmatic, preliminary demonstration of the phials' structural behaviour rather than a comprehensive material modelling campaign. Nevertheless, with appropriate adjustments and considerations, these results provide a reliable foundation for the subsequent steps in the airbag design workflow.
