\section{Step 2 - Axial compression test (FEM)}\label{section:step2-fem-compression}
The quantity of interest for the calibration of the glass phial's mechanical behaviour is the \textit{tensile strength} \texttt{FT} of the glass material card in LS-DYNA (\kw{MAT\_GLASS}, corresponding to the material model \kw{MAT\_280}), which governs the failure load observed in the present FEM model. As the model was designed to simulate as closely as possible the loading condition to which the phials were subjected in Step 1 of the workflow, the parameter \texttt{FT} is tuned iteratively until the failure force predicted by the FEM matches the value of 98.7\,N measured experimentally. The calibrated model is then used to characterise the behaviour of the same glass phials in a separate FEM model in Step 3, aimed at predicting the maximum impact acceleration that the phials can withstand. This value is one of the key design parameters for sizing the airbag protection device. 

The model is built using \textit{explicit} time integration. The unit system used in the present model is as follows:

\subsection{Implementation}
\subsubsection{Model setup and glass material modelling}
% geometry (full model), components, mesh, contact, boundary conditions

The model is composed of three parts, as shown in \cref{fig:fem-model-setup}: the \textit{base}, the \textit{crosshead} and the \textit{glass phial}. The numerical setup aims to imitate the experimental test, where the glass phial is compressed in the axial direction, i.e. the vertical (Y) direction. Two considerations need to be made regarding the construction of the model:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The glass phials virtual twin was reproduced on the CAD software SolidWorks by taking manual (destructive) measurements on the specimens offered by the Molinette Hospital. As the phials' geometry contains curvatures, fillets, domes, and bulges, using a simple Vernier calliper certainly lowers the fidelity of the virtual representation and induces challenges in the evaluation of the numerical results;
  \item The absence of the PETG holder in the present numerical model is a decision rooted in the risk of inaccurate or excessively complex modelling, as it was 3D printed with a reticular filling that would require a destructive intervention to be observed. The consequences of omitting the PETG adaptor in the FEM model are another aspect that is explored and accounted for in the results presented later.
\end{enumerate}

Within the FEM model, the crosshead and the base belong to the same super-part, named \textit{machine}, and they are meshed identically. Both are made of solid hexa elements with an average side length of 0.2\,mm, extruded to a thickness of 1\,mm in the vertical direction. In plan, they measure 18 x 18\,mm (base) and 14 x 14\,mm (crosshead). Both have enough surface area to cover the base and the dome of the glass phial, so that the breaking pressure and the reaction force from the base are applied correctly.

The two parts are modelled with a material that treats them as infinitely rigid bodies (\kw{MAT\_RIGID}, material model \kw{MAT\_020}), with only one free degree of freedom, in this case the displacement in the Y-direction. The base is then constrained in Y by a boundary condition that imposes a displacement of 0\,mm, while the crosshead moves downwards and applies pressure on the phial in a quasi-static regime. The numerical workaround of leaving the Y direction free and then imposing a zero-displacement boundary condition on the base makes it possible to measure the reaction force exerted on it in the direction of the degree of freedom that was chosen as liable. With a fully constrained rigid body, this measurement would not be so straightforward, needing a slightly different procedure. The material card applied to the machine's parts can be found in \cref{lst:mat-rigid}.

The glass phial is meshed with shell elements with \texttt{ELFORM = 2}\footnote{Reduced-integration elements with one in-plane integration point and through-thickness points chosen manually, with the default of two. No hourglass control implemented.} of thickness 0.4\,mm, with 5 integration points along the thickness. The structured mesh has a regular size of 0.1\,mm and is mostly composed of quads, with trias where needed to follow the shape, so that it keeps the curvatures of the dome and the fillets of the rounded-corner geometry. The material used for the glass phial is shown in \cref{lst:mat-glass-raw}, which highlights the \textit{tensile strength} \texttt{FT} as the parameter to fine-tune the numerical prediction of the phial's failure load. The card has several parameters of interest that govern the post-fracture behaviour, which is not of interest for the present analysis as the objective is to identify the material's point of brittle failure, aligned with the broader aim to avoid medicinal dispersion during an impact-failure event. However, an informed attempt to model glass behaviour with an acceptable degree of fidelity in both the pre- and post-fracture regimes was made by setting material parameters based on a separate literature search across worked examples of \kw{MAT\_GLASS} implementations \cite{DCFEA2025} and the LS-DYNA user manual \cite[p.~1989-2001]{LSDYNA_KW_II}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{RHO}: Density, 2.6E-06 kg/mm$^3$ (2600 kg/m$^3$);
  \item \texttt{E}: Young\'s modulus, 70 GPa, a relatively well-established value for commercial soda-lime glass;
  \item \texttt{PR}: Poisson\'s ratio, 0.23, chosen within a range between 0.20 to 0.25 for soda-lime glass; 
  \item \texttt{ILAW}: Flag to choose the degradation procedure (among two options, 0 and 1) when critical stress is reached. The default value of 0 signifies that the family of parameters relative to \texttt{NCYCR} (found in lines 8-9 of the material card) have to be defined;
  \item \texttt{FMOD}: Flag to choose between failure criteria (among six options). The default value of 0 is chosen, selecting a \textit{Rankine maximum stress} criterion\footnote{The \textit{Rankine Maximum Stress} criterion consists in bounding principal stresses, $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, between tensile strength, $f_t$, and compressive strength, $f_c$, as follows: $-f_c < \{\sigma_1, \sigma_2\} < f_t$. It is the least complex maximum stress criterion available in LS-DYNA.};
  \item \texttt{FT}: Tensile strength, knob to fine-tune the glass phial's failure load, expressed in GPa. Common values found in literature span from 40\,MPa to 70\,MPa. 
  \item \texttt{FC}: Compressive strength, usually one order of magnitude greater than the tensile strength in glass, here arbitrarily chosen to 1\,GPa to guarantee tensile-strength driven failure when fine-tuning the parameter FT;
  \item \texttt{FTSCL}: Scale factor for the tensile strength (where the default = 1.0). $FT_{mod} = FTSCL \times FT$, thus, in this case, the glass' actual tensile strength needs to be scaled by the chosen factor of FTSCL = 1.2. An effective value of tensile strength $FT_{mod} > FT$ implies that the tensile strength drops to its original value of FT as soon as the first crack happens in the phial. Although it adds numerical complexity, having FTSCL > 1.0 can be helpful in modelling high-force peaks in impact events according to the LS-DYNA user manual, which suits the intended application of all the FEM models produced in this thesis;   
  \item \texttt{SFSTI} and \texttt{SFSTR}: Scaling factors for elastic stiffness and stress after failure, respectively, to be applied at the end of the post-crack softening. It dictates how much residual stress the element can hold after it has been damaged, scaling the post-crack strength and stiffness down to SF-XXX $\times$ FT or E. In this case, SFSTI = SFSTR = 0.01 means that both stiffness and stress are reduced to 1\% of their values at failure; 
  \item \texttt{ICRIN}: Flag for crack strain initialisation. The default value of 0 indicates that initial crack strain is the strain reached at failure;
  \item \texttt{NCYCR}: Number of cycles in which the stress is reduced to SFSTR $\times$ failure stress, in this case chosen to 50.
  \item \texttt{NIPF}: Number of failed through-thickness integration points needed to label the element as failed, in this case chosen as 5 to consider all through-thickness integration points set in the phial's shell elements;
  \item \texttt{EPSCR}: The effective critical strain to trigger element deletion, which can be useful to eliminate highly distorted elements. However, this value was set to the extremely high value of 0.1 to purposefully avoid premature element deletion and having to implement an internal surface generation criterion for contact modelling.
  \item \texttt{CTRACK}: Flag for crack tracking algorithm, set to the non-default value of 1 to track cracks. 
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Boundary conditions}
The phial simply rests on the base's surface. Its interaction with the base and the crosshead is governed by the contact card \kw{CONTACT\_AUTOMATIC\_SINGLE\_SURFACE\_ID}, the most general contact model in LS-DYNA, as shown in \cref{lst:contact-single-surface}. It allows all surfaces to come in contact with each other without defining single master-slave pairs. The tangential behaviour is defined by a friction coefficient of 0.3 for both static (\texttt{FS}) and dynamic (\texttt{FD}) friction. The phial is liable and only feels the reaction force from below and the force applied by the moving crosshead.

A velocity-control boundary condition is applied to the crosshead in the vertical direction Y to induce a total displacement of approximately 0.2\,mm. Velocity is applied with an initial ramp that scales up to a constant negative velocity of 0.0266\,mm/ms. The choice of imposing the motion of the crosshead through a velocity boundary condition, rather than the more usual displacement boundary condition, is rooted in the idea of emulating the experiment, in which the test machine moves the crosshead at a constant, relatively slow speed of 1 to 3\,mm/min. Furthermore, velocity cannot be applied as a step as an instantaneous jump from zero to the final velocity would correspond to an infinite acceleration at the beginning of the analysis. In an explicit simulation, this impulse would be transmitted to the phial through the contact as a numerically unwelcome spike in the reaction force, followed by spurious oscillations that could potentially even trigger premature glass failure. With the initial ramp, the velocity increases linearly, so the acceleration remains finite and non-zero only during the ramp. The application velocity is low enough to avoid a fully dynamic regime, as explained in the modal analysis section, but still higher than in the actual experiment, as applying a similar loading speed would increase the runtime excessively due to the stability condition imposed on the timestep.

The first elastic mode (Mode 7) is of particular relevance for the present analysis. It is a bending mode, with a natural frequency of about 8.179~\,kHz, and its period $T_1$ is the characteristic time of the structure's dynamic response:

\begin{equation}
T_1 = 1/f_1 \approx 122~\mu\mathrm{s}
\end{equation}

If the load were applied over a time of the same order as $T_1$, the structure's inertia would contribute to the response and the test would fall in the dynamic regime. Therefore, to keep the simulation quasi-static, the load application time must be at least ten times $T_1$, i.e.\ above about 1.22\,ms. For this reason, the crosshead was driven by a velocity curve that ramps up over 5\,ms to a constant value and then holds it for the remaining 5\,ms of the simulation. The ramp time is therefore about 40 times the period of the first elastic mode, well inside the quasi-static range. 

\subsubsection{Geometric imperfections}
The phials tested experimentally showed a large dispersion in their strength properties, as well as in their breaking modes. This phenomenon is usually ascribed to the loosely controlled manufacturing process (mentioned in \cref{section:step1-test}) which naturally introduces geometric imperfections. These imperfections are not influential in the context of the medical applications for which the phials were designed, but are certainly exposed in destructive testing such as what was adopted in Step 1. For these reasons, it was necessary to introduce initial defects into the FEM model by perturbing the phial's geometry. Two options were considered:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Perturbing the nodes along a primarily bending (see Mode 18 in \cref{fig:phial-modes}) mode shape from the modal analysis, as to introduce a specific defect of controlled amplitude (measured in mm) over the nominal geometry;
  \item Leaving the geometry unchanged and rotating the phial by $1^\circ$ about its centroid in the XY-plane, which introduces a misalignment between the phial axis and the loading direction (as shown in \cref{fig:phial-misalignment})
\end{enumerate}

After a series of iterations verifying the numerically observed breaking mode, the second option was chosen as it was found that this misalignment triggers an additional, hybrid breaking mode, which had also been observed experimentally. The results presented later therefore confirm a correlation between initial deviations from the nominal geometry and the different breaking modes observed in the tests.


\subsubsection{Requested outputs}
The monitored outputs are the displacement of the crosshead and the displacement of the glass node on top of the dome (monitored through the \texttt{NODOUT} card), the reaction forces on both the crosshead and the base (\texttt{RCFORC} card), added mass/energy data (\texttt{GLSTAT, MATSUM} cards), and the \texttt{MAT\_GLASS} \textit{Extra History Variable \#1}, with an index ($D \in [0, 1]$) stored in the \texttt{d3plot} contour file to visualise the damage progression and crack contour patterns across the glass phial. To obtain results comparable with the experimental campaign, a force-displacement curve was extracted from each run of the simulation, using the reaction force on the crosshead and the displacement of the glass node at the top of the phial dome (considering its displacement only until its eventual brittle failure). The objective is to reproduce the experimentally-observed brittle failure, in which the glass behaves linearly elastically up to fracture, with no plastic deformation before it breaks.


\subsubsection{Calibration procedure}
As shown in the \cref{lst:mat-glass-raw}, the tensile strength \texttt{FT} is the parameter used as a calibration knob to adjust the breaking point of the phial until it matches the failure load value measured experimentally at 98.7\,N (5th percentile of the Batch-A Weibull distribution of failure loads). Therefore, several simulations were run iteratively testing different values of \texttt{FT} until the simulated phial broke within a \hl{1\%} tolerance from the experimental failure load. The resulting value of \texttt{FT} should therefore be read as a calibrated model parameter rather than as the actual tensile strength of the glass, since it also absorbs the effects of mesh size, imperfections and misalignment. This calibrated material model characterises the phial and is used as input to the next step of the workflow.

The two graphs in \cref{fig:step2-ft-calibration} show the load-displacement curves for mode A and mode AD as parameter \texttt{FT} varies. Each curve shows an initial soft branch indicating a transient phase in which machine-phial contact fully engages, followed by a linear elastic behaviour up to the brittle failure point. Since the phial's stiffness is naturally independent of FT, the failure points of the different curves lie along the same slope, at increasing loads for increasing values of FT. For mode A, a value of $\mathrm{FT} = 60$\,MPa gives a breaking load of 96.9\,N, compared with 98.7\,N established experimentally. While at first sight mode AD points to a different \texttt{FT} value to match the 98.7\,N failure threshold, it can be useful to isolate the load-displacement curves with $\mathrm{FT} = 60$\,MPa for both mode A and mode AD, as illustrated in \cref{fig:step2-ft-calibration-overlay}. The FE model captures the hybrid failure mode AD remarkably well: initial fracture occurs at the base due to tilting-induced stress concentrations, but the structure retains partial load-bearing capacity until ultimate failure occurs at a load comparable to the dome failure in Mode A. This aligns with the first-hand laboratory experience concerning the hybrid mode AD, which was characterized by distinct acoustic emissions: a minor energy release during initial base fracture, followed by a much larger release upon final dome collapse. 

Overall, it is possible to conclude that the calibrated value of $F_T = 60\,\text{MPa}$ successfully reproduces the experimental behaviour for Batch A, closely aligning with the $5^{\text{th}}$ percentile estimate derived in \cref{section:step1-test}. While this value falls within the typical literature range ($45\text{-}70\text{ MPa}$) \hl{CITE HERE!!!!!!}, using a value from such a low percentile suggests that the true material strength is likely higher. The fact that a low tensile strength is required to match the experimental load suggests that the modelled phial geometry may be overly thin, as a thicker geometry would offer greater compressive resistance. The combination of thin wall and low breaking load is therefore a generally conservative reproduction of phial behaviour, which suits the purposes of this workflow. 

As noted previously, these results should not be interpreted as a rigorous material characterisation. The testing conditions were non-standard, including a custom adaptor that required stiffness calibration and a batch-to-batch variability stemming directly from differences in pharmaceutical phial manufacturing parameters. Furthermore, this evaluation was strictly limited to axial compression, omitting other critical loading cases such as pure bending or direct tension, which would typically exhibit even higher scatter in failure strength. Ultimately, this testing served as a pragmatic, preliminary demonstration of the phials' structural behaviour rather than a comprehensive material modelling campaign. Nevertheless, with appropriate adjustments and considerations, these results provide a reliable foundation for the subsequent steps in the airbag design workflow.
