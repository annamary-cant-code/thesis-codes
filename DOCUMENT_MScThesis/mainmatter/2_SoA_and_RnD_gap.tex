\chapter{Background and Research-Development Gap}
\label{chapter:SoA-and-RnD}

In technology development, it is important to gain a clear idea of two aspects:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Research aspect}: if the technology that is being developed requires the exploration of an uncharted research niche, as opposed to utilising pre-existing and consolidated knowledge for the creation of a new product;
    \item \textbf{Development aspect}: whether the proposed technology targets a well-defined market with unmet customer demand and no established supply.
\end{enumerate} 

This chapter explores both these aspects in Sections \ref{section:payload-first-approach}-\ref{section:airbag-design-variables} and \ref{section:market-analysis}, respectively. The aim is to gather sufficient background information, from market allocation of the proposed technology to concrete understanding of airbag design criteria, to depict the choices proposed in Chapter \ref{chapter:methodology} regarding operative \textit{methodology} as an answer to the Research Questions proposed at the end of this chapter.


\section{Regional Market Analysis: medical UAV logistics in Piedmont}\label{section:market-analysis}

Adopting a region-based, rather than a nation-based, approach for market analysis is, therefore, necessary. Table \ref{tab:actors-piedmont} highlights stakeholders involved in Piedmont-based operations and their roles in the value chain, illustrating a fragmented scenario: Project INDOOR, launched by Fondazione DOT in 2021, culminated in a 500\,m-long test flight by an autonomous UAV between two Città della Salute hospital sites, carrying medical goods in a dedicated capsule, on 1st October 2024. In this project, several other stakeholders were involved (in fact, most of the ones present in Table \ref{tab:actors-piedmont}), among which ABzero (a spin-off from \textit{Scuola Normale Superiore di Pisa} responsible for capsule design and handling the ENAC authorisation process for the test flight), parallel research group PIC4SeR from the Department of Electronics and Telecommunications at \textit{Politecnico di Torino}, and \textit{MAVTech S.r.l.} (UAV systems designer company based in Torino). Independently of the INDOOR project of 2021-2024, the startup SkyEU was founded in 2025 promising to develop eVTOL drone systems for critical logistics within, but not limited to, the healthcare sector.

In parallel, and independently of INDOOR, the research group led by Prof. Enrico Cestino at the Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering (DIMEAS) of \textit{Politecnico di Torino}, together with \textit{A.O.U. Città della Salute e della Scienza} and \textit{FEV Italia}, has pursued since 2020 a series of feasibility studies on the Molinette--San Giovanni Bosco route (Section \ref{section:prior-research}). These covered regulatory and mission evaluation, risk mapping and path planning, payload environmental loads, and cost--safety comparisons between road, drone and eVTOL transport. No flight campaign has been carried out so far. This makes the group the only Piedmontese actor whose work so far has been entirely at the planning and assessment level.

On the other hand, the regional administration's general stance with respect to this type of operation highlights the fundamental requirement of thorough benefit-risk analyses to guarantee both the safety of the Piedmontese citizens and the preservation of the medical cargo, possibly needing to limit transportation to exceptional emergencies in mountain areas \cite{cr_piemonte}. From an engineering perspective, these criticalities can be translated in the following barriers for the development of a full-scale, UAV-based delivery network:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Authorisation of flights}: Operations beyond the open category are approved by compiling the SORA. Among the different classes of mitigations, \textit{ground risk} mitigations (one of the first steps required in the SORA) reduce the consequences of the UAV's impact on the ground after a loss of control, for example by introducing a parachute. It is key for technical stakeholders to prove the reliability of their implemented ground risk mitigations to protect civilians in case of drone failure.
    \item \textbf{Payload integrity and containment}: In accordance with the regional administration's concerns, current certification standard UN3373 for ground handling of Biological Substances, Category B, establishes that packages must pass a drop test at 1.2m with no leakage from the primary container \hl{CITE STANDARD HERE}. However, in the event of a free-fall from ordinary mission altitudes, this standard loses its significance and the potential for biological material dispersion into the environment becomes highly relevant.  
    \item \textbf{Unsustainability of the current business models} While costs of road and helicopter transport of biomedical goods such as organs, drugs and other critical supplies are expected to fall significantly with drones, most trials are funded by EU projects and one-time grants, with the consequence of making the project unsustainable and non-scalable.
\end{enumerate}


On the basis of this analysis, several research and development gaps can be identified: SkyEU is endeavouring to enact an economically sustainable business model for large-scale UAV networks, but has a small public record; ABzero has patented capsules equipped with electronics that reliably monitor in-flight vibrations and guarantee thermal control of the medical payload, but these are only certified to the UN3373 standard, whereas in case of in-flight failure a much more conservative approach is needed; regional authorities need reassurance on the safety aspect for civilians, as a 500\,m test flight on a controlled route shows that the obstacle is not drone-making, but guaranteeing safety in everyday performance rather than in single monitored missions.

In this scenario, the institutions (\textit{Politecnico di Torino (DIMEAS), FEV Italia, AOU Città della Salute}) that commissioned the present thesis project aim to tackle, among the three barriers, barrier 2, concerning payload integrity and containment. The proposed solution is an airbag-type device that protects the payload and prevents its dispersion in the event of an impact, especially in view of transporting extremely valuable medicinals and/or biological materials, e.g. human organs for urgent transplant operations. This device is conceived as an add-on to existing capsules such as the one by ABzero, mounted on drones such as SkyEU's, and is intended to help close the certification gap highlighted above, while acknowledging that it does not address the issues linked to SORA ground risk or the sustainability of the business model.



\section{Airframe-first vs. Safety-first: a design-philosophy gap}\label{section:payload-first-approach}

This section builds on the research-development gap presented earlier by attempting to connect its root causes to a discrepancy between design philosophies applied in the aeronautical and the biomedical sectors. The proposed design solution (the airbag-type device) is, in fact, a suggestion to solve an "aeronautical problem" implementing a "medical approach".

The issue of proving compliance to match demanding safety standards imposed by the certification authorities does not appear to be a Piedmont-only (or even an Italy-only) problem, but rather a widespread trend that affects all non-standard aeronautical projects across the field. As highlighted, for example, in \cite{chhetri2026survey}, most UAV producers focus on optimising their aerial vehicle's design, minimising mission-related vibrational loads, thermal loads, overall mass, but also proposing ever more efficient cargo-drop mechanisms and ways to overcome geographical obstacles, all partaking in a feedback loop against aerodynamic and flight-mechanics performance metrics. This machine-centric approach appears to be the default, especially considering that UAV-based missions are, naturally, radically different from standard airline and/or general aviation operations, where \hl{the safety of the passengers is undistinguishable from the safety of the vehicle} and where, therefore, stringent certification requirements strongly limit design freedom \hl{CITE SOMETHING HERE?}. A common consequence of focusing exclusively on technological potential when designing UAV-based operations is, therefore, neglecting the safety assurance aspect of the project, which is usually delegated to including a parachute-type device in the final design phases. As pointed out by \cite{alulema2026how} in their design feasibility evaluation study, the introduction of a parachute usually increases the risk of falling into a mass-increase feedback loop: the parachute increases the airframe's overall mass, which then requires a larger, thus heavier, parachute for efficient deceleration during free-fall, which again results in an excessive mass increase, and so on. This negative feedback loop results either in never reaching design convergence, or otherwise in having to accept a smaller parachute with reduced deceleration capacity, still implying a relatively high ground-impact velocity and almost certainly destructive damage to the airframe, its cargo, and the impacted areas on the ground.     

The general impression given by the brief market analysis conducted earlier and the observations gathered in the present paragraph is that, perhaps, the "airframe-first" approach adopted in the majority of \hl{nascenti? appena nuove?} UAV-based operations is not sustainable for the projects' longevity beyond pilot flights, and that it might be necessary to embrace a paradigm shift in design approaches. Specifically, there might be potential in drawing inspiration from the biomedical field, where medical technology designers are encouraged to shift from a techno-centric to a human-centred approach to avoid the \textit{translation gap} or \textit{implementation gap}, i.e. the potential to introduce or enhance health inequity when trying to implement extremely advanced technology that does not take into account all necessities and limitations of human patients \cite{rodriguez2023thinking}. 

Just like success is measured on the patient and not on the device in the biomedical field, in the case of UAV-based medical delivery operations success should be measured on payload integrity and protection of civilians, not (only) on the capabilities of the airframe. A drone that survives an in-flight failure while sacrificing its payload and threatening irreversible human damage has undoubtedly failed its mission, as for a biomedical device that functions correctly but does not serve the patient. This reasoning leads to the first of the contributions proposed in the present thesis, which is the \textit{"safety-first"} design principle presented in Section \ref{section:outer-design-loop}. This thesis aims to serve as a test-bed for this proposed approach, where exploring the airbag design process helps bridge the gap between current development efforts and certification.


\section{Airbag impact attenuation: background and design variables}\label{section:airbag-design-variables}
Airbag-type devices have a well-established, enduring research-development record with contributions found across multiple industries. The information presented in Sections \ref{section:airbag-historical-perspective} and \ref{section:airbag-design-guidelines} aims to represent the State of the Art practices adopted in airbag design. The scope of this investigation is developing a solid, although perhaps not comprehensive, understanding of design recommendations on which to base the proposal for the design workflow of later Section \ref{section:inner-design-loop}.

\subsection{Established applications and common working principle}\label{section:airbag-historical-perspective}
Airbag technology is well established across two primary sectors: \textit{automotive} and \textit{aerospace}.

In the automotive industry, these systems are implemented during vehicle crashes, distinguishing between general automobile crashes and motorcycle impacts. In the space industry, ever since the early space missions of the 1960s, airbags have been deployed during the final landing phases of space capsules. Within the aeronautical industry, specifically in the "specific" operations category, other niche applications have emerged, such as the experimental study of adaptive airbag systems for the protection of UAVs and general aviation vehicles \cite{HolnickiSzulc2023} (following a long track record of exploration in the field of adaptive technologies for impact absorption \cite{HolnickiSzulc2015, HolnickiSzulc2009}). 

In either case, the primary objective of the airbag-type device remains the same: to be stored compactly in ordinary mission conditions, inflate within the allowable time before impact (whether during an emergency impact or landing), and decelerate the target body (whether a crash test dummy or a spacecraft) far more gently than in case of direct impact. When analysing these systems, purely analytical, hard-codable solutions are typically limited to single-degree-of-freedom (1-DoF) formulations, immediately resolving to the use of far more computationally-heavy finite element analyses to capture the complex two- and three-dimensional effects of the impact. The only exception to this trend is the technical report NATICK/TR-87/042 published by T. F. Patterson in 1985\footnote{T. F. Patterson, \textit{Design, Fabrication and Testing of an Airdrop Platform Utilizing Airbags as Shock Absorbers}, NATICK/TR-87/042, U.S. Army Natick Research, Development and Engineering Center, Aug. 1985.}, which presents a 6-DoF analytical simulation built on closed-form component models, but remains inaccessible at the time of writing of the present literature search.

Despite the diversity of fields, all applications share identical fundamental working principles. An example of an analytical study of airbag-based impact events, and one of the most significant papers in shaping this thesis' work, is Zhou et al. (2019) \cite{Zhou2019}, in turn heavily rooted in the SAND93-2133 technical report \cite{Cole1993} on a code for predicting the performance of the Pathfinder lander's airbag impact attenuation system, which describes the core working principle of airbag energy dissipation in a one-degree-of-freedom impact event.

\section{Research-Development Objective}

Having presented the context in which the research project is allocated and having gathered the necessary design information, it is possible to address the requirements set by the project commissioners:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and characterise the mechanical allowables of typical medical payloads based on general packaging requirements for UAV-transported medical goods.
    \item Develop an informed conceptual design of an airbag system capable of deploying during emergency release conditions to absorb impact energy and preserve payload integrity.
\end{itemize}

These requirements can be summarised in the following Research Objective and Research Questions:

\begin{objectivebox}{Research-Development Objective}
Develop and validate a design workflow to model and simulate an airbag-based protective system for UAV medical payloads using LS-DYNA, with the primary objective to ensure the structural integrity of pharmaceutical materials and their containment during drone failure and emergency payload release, thereby preventing environmental dispersion and supporting certification requirements.
\end{objectivebox}

\begin{objectivebox}{Research Question(s)}
How can a systematic multi-phase simulation workflow be developed to design an airbag-based protective system for medical payloads during UAV emergency-release events?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the allowable mechanical limits (acceleration, stress, etc.) for medical products during emergency release, and how should these define the design envelope?
    \item How can the inflated airbag configuration be optimised to satisfy impact-absorption requirements while balancing competing design requirements?
    \item How can the optimised inflated geometry be transformed into a compact and functionally deployable system?
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}
